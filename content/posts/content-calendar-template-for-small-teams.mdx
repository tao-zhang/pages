Four posts a week sounds aggressive until you have a system for it.
Without one, it turns into either burnout or filler content just to hit
the number. Here's the calendar structure we actually run.

## The core columns

Every post in the calendar gets tracked with:

- **Target publish date** — a specific day, not "sometime this week."
  Vague deadlines are the easiest way to lose a cadence.
- **Title** — working title is fine, but write one before you start
  drafting. It forces you to know what the post is actually about.
- **Pillar** — which content bucket this belongs to (for us: SEO
  fundamentals, content ops, AI-assisted marketing, or build-in-public).
  Rotating pillars across the week keeps the calendar from feeling
  repetitive.
- **Keyword/intent** — the specific search intent this post targets. See
  our guide to [keyword research without paid
  tools](/blog/keyword-research-without-paid-tools) if you don't have a
  paid research tool yet.
- **Status** — planned, drafted, in review, published. Four stages, no
  more — a complicated status system is itself a productivity tax.

## Plan further out than you publish

We plan roughly 8–9 weeks ahead at the topic level, but only fully draft
1–2 weeks ahead of the publish date. This does two things: it guarantees
you're never scrambling for a topic the morning something's due, while
keeping the actual writing close enough to publish date that it stays
timely and reflects anything you've learned from recently published
posts.

## Batch by pillar, not by date

Writing four completely different types of posts back-to-back is slower
than it needs to be — the context-switching cost is real. Where possible,
draft posts from the same pillar in the same sitting (e.g., two SEO
fundamentals posts in one session), even if they'll publish on different
days.

## Build in a review buffer

A post drafted the same day it's due to publish has no room for a second
look. Aim to have a post fully drafted at least 2–3 days before its
target date, so there's time for a read-through, an editing pass, and
(where the workflow requires it) an actual review step before it goes
live — see our [on-page SEO checklist](/blog/on-page-seo-checklist) as
the pre-publish pass we run on everything.

## Revisit the calendar weekly, not just when it breaks

Once you have any performance data, spend ten minutes a week checking
which pillars and topics are actually landing. Underperforming topics get
deprioritized in future weeks; overperforming ones get more of the
calendar. A content calendar that never changes based on results isn't a
strategy, it's a to-do list.

## The template, minimal version

If you want the simplest possible version: one spreadsheet, one row per
post, five columns (date, title, pillar, keyword/intent, status). Add
complexity only when the simple version actually breaks for you — most
calendars fail from being too elaborate to maintain, not too simple.
