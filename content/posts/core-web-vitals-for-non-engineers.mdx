You don't need to be an engineer to understand Core Web Vitals, but most
explanations are written like you already are one. Here's the version
for anyone whose job is the content, not the codebase.

## What they are, in plain terms

Core Web Vitals are three specific measurements Google uses as part of
how it evaluates page experience — and page experience is a (modest but
real) ranking factor.

- **Largest Contentful Paint (LCP)** — how long it takes for the main
  content of a page to actually appear on screen. If a reader clicks your
  result and stares at a blank or half-loaded page for a few seconds,
  that's a bad LCP, and it costs you readers before they've read a word.
- **Interaction to Next Paint (INP)** — how quickly the page responds
  once someone actually tries to interact with it (tapping a link,
  opening a menu). A page that "looks" loaded but is unresponsive when
  touched fails this one.
- **Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS)** — how much content unexpectedly
  jumps around while loading (an image popping in and shoving the text
  you were reading down the page). This is the one every reader has
  experienced and hated, even without knowing its name.

## Why a content person should care

You're not going to fix these directly — that's squarely the engineering
team's job, and it's a deliberate boundary here, not an oversight. But two
things are worth knowing:

1. **Heavy embeds and large, uncompressed images you drop into a post can
   hurt all three metrics**, even on an otherwise well-optimized site. An
   oversized hero image is a content decision with a technical
   consequence.
2. **These scores are a shared responsibility, not just a platform
   concern.** If a post's Core Web Vitals look worse than the rest of the
   site, it's worth flagging — as this checklist mentions under image
   guidance in our [on-page SEO checklist](/blog/on-page-seo-checklist),
   before assuming it's purely a platform issue.

## What "good" looks like

Google publishes rough thresholds: LCP under ~2.5 seconds, INP under
~200 milliseconds, CLS under 0.1. You don't need to memorize the exact
numbers — you need to recognize the pattern: fast to appear, fast to
respond, and visually stable while it loads.

## How to check without engineering help

Google's free PageSpeed Insights tool (search for it, paste in a URL) will
score any live page against all three metrics and flag likely causes.
It's a two-minute check worth running occasionally on your highest-traffic
posts, even if the fix itself belongs to engineering.

## The takeaway

Core Web Vitals aren't a writer's job to fix, but they're worth
understanding well enough to know when a slow-loading post might be
costing you readers — and to ask the right question of the right person
when it does.
