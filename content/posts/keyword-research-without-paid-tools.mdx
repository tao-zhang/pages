Paid keyword tools are genuinely useful — but "we don't have one yet"
isn't a reason to skip keyword research, just a reason to do it
differently. Here's the method we're using ourselves.

## Start from real questions, not guesses

The best free keyword research source is the search engine itself, used
deliberately:

- **Autocomplete.** Type your topic into the search box and note what it
  suggests — those are real, frequent queries, not hypotheticals.
- **"People also ask" boxes.** Each one is a validated question real
  people search, often with sub-questions you wouldn't have thought of.
- **Related searches at the bottom of the results page.** These map the
  surrounding topic cluster, which is useful for planning follow-up posts
  once you've written the main one.

## Read the search results, not just the queries

Search your candidate keyword and look at what's already ranking:

- **What format wins?** A checklist, a comparison table, a long
  narrative guide — the top results tell you what searchers and the
  algorithm currently consider a satisfying answer.
- **What's missing?** If every result is generic or thin, that's an
  opening. If every result is a well-resourced, in-depth guide from an
  established site, that keyword may not be realistic yet for a new
  domain — deprioritize it in favor of a more specific, longer-tail
  version of the same question.

## Favor specific over broad

"SEO tips" is a keyword a brand-new site has almost no chance of ranking
for — it's dominated by sites with years of authority. "On-page SEO
checklist" or "how to write a meta description" are more specific, lower
competition, and still genuinely useful searches. Specificity is a
feature, not a compromise, when you're starting from zero domain
authority.

## Use your own product/audience knowledge

Free tools can't tell you what your specific audience actually struggles
with — but you probably already know. The questions your users or
customers ask you directly are keyword research, even if no tool
validates the search volume. Write those down before you ever open a
search engine.

## Keep a simple running list

A single spreadsheet works: one column for the keyword/question, one for
the search intent (informational, how-to, comparison), one for status
(planned/drafted/published). This becomes the input for your [content
calendar](/blog/content-calendar-template-for-small-teams) — and once a
paid tool is available, it's a fast retrofit to add real volume and
difficulty data rather than a process you have to build from scratch.

## When to upgrade

Free methods get you a solid first few months of topics. Once you have
published posts and some traffic data, that data (which posts perform,
which don't) becomes more valuable than most keyword tools anyway —
you're no longer guessing at intent, you're reading it directly from
[our on-page SEO checklist](/blog/on-page-seo-checklist) applied to real
results.
